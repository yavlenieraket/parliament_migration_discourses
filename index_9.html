Method: how the dashboard connects the Python and LLM stages

The first analysis stage was deterministic and corpus-wide. We extracted country/entity mentions, mapped target countries, scored concreteness, classified migration directions, migrant cohorts, policy measures, sentiment, and policy-agency labels. The earlier HTML index visualized those features as attention matrices, country signatures, over-represented targets, and similarity maps. Those Python outputs showed where the corpus had meaningful structure, but they could not answer why an asymmetric relation existed or what rhetorical move a country mention performed.

Scope correction. Reciprocal asymmetry is only claimed where both countries have parliamentary corpus data. Mentions of Syria, Afghanistan, Libya, Morocco, and similar countries without source-parliament data in this project are treated as external target salience, not asymmetry. In the downloaded Task 1 output, 6 rows are therefore excluded as external target salience, and 0 additional reciprocal rows are excluded from the asymmetry charts because the forward evidence is below 30 mentions.

Normalization correction. Reciprocal asymmetry is now evaluated on attention rates rather than raw counts: target-country mentions divided by the source parliament's analyzed token volume when token counts are available, otherwise by total country-mention volume. Rates are reported per 1,000 normalization units. This prevents larger corpora from looking asymmetric simply because they contain more text. Raw counts remain in the hover text as context, but the normalized ratio is the primary evidence.

The second stage used LLM prompts on LUMI to add interpretive labels to selected high-value slices: asymmetry drivers, concrete events, model/pressure positioning, cross-target summaries, domestic migration framing, policy convergence relations, and argumentative schemes. The charts below keep those two layers together: the Python layer explains what pushed us toward the LLM tasks; the LLM layer gives the qualitative interpretation.

1. Corpus parsingParlaMint speeches were transformed into country/entity mention rows.
2. Python featuresConcreteness, sentiment, direction, cohorts, policies, and attention matrices were computed.
3. Pattern selectionAsymmetric dyads, high-concreteness mentions, and policy-similar pairs were selected.
4. LUMI promptsLLM tasks classified drivers, frames, policy relations, and argumentative schemes.
5. SynthesisCross-tabs, maps, heatmaps, and summaries turn labels into paper-ready findings.

Full data pipeline: from corpus to prompts

How a 25-parliament corpus becomes interpreted evidence.

Method ParlaMint 2 transcripts are parsed into per-mention rows; Python extracts deterministic features (concreteness, sentiment, direction, cohorts, policy measures, target distributions). An LLM stage then receives only analytically selected slices of this matrix, not the full corpus.
Theoretical implication This separation matters epistemologically: the LLM is not a corpus reader, it is a labeller of pre-computed patterns. Reproducibility is anchored at the Python feature stage; the LUMI prompts add interpretive depth to features the analyst has already verified.

Key takeaway

Why two stages, not one

Every chart in this dashboard can be traced to a Python feature column and, where relevant, to a specific LUMI prompt task. Numbers come from the deterministic layer; rhetorical interpretation comes from the LLM layer.

Findings · 0

What the earlier Python visualizations showed

Why the LLM stage was needed: the descriptive layer alone could not answer the rhetorical questions.

Method The earlier HTML atlas computed, for each pair of parliaments, raw mention counts, over-represented target lists, and country-signature tables across concreteness, cohorts, and policy measures.
Finding The corpus is empirically lopsided. A handful of parliaments (Netherlands, UK, Belgium) account for outsized mention volume; one or two target countries dominate each source parliament's attention geography (Greece for Iceland with 17% of all mentions; Libya for the UK at 23%; Syria for Turkey at 23%).
Theoretical implication This unevenness is the analytical engine for the LLM stage. Once we know that Iceland concentrates 17% of its country-mentions on Greece, the next question — what kind of rhetorical relation that produces — cannot be answered by Python features alone. The LLM layer was designed exactly for that second question.

Python signals that motivated the LLM pass

Why structure alone is not enough

Case study · 1

Asymmetric attention: who talks about whom, and why

After normalization, the strongest asymmetries are not where raw counts suggested.

Method For every directed pair of parliaments with at least 30 forward mentions and where both sides are in the project corpus, Qwen2.5-7B received the forward and reverse mention rates per 1,000 context tokens together with representative snippets, and was prompted to assign one of four asymmetry drivers.
What we measure Normalized attention ratio = source mention rate of the target ÷ reverse mention rate, both per 1,000 context tokens. External targets without project parliament data (Syria, Afghanistan, Libya, Morocco) are excluded from the asymmetry claim and routed to target-salience and cross-target framing analyses instead.
Finding After normalization, the most asymmetric reciprocal pairs are Iceland → Greece (ratio 1,406) and Czechia → Turkey (860). Eighteen of the top thirty asymmetries are accounted for by Crisis Visibility; Border Proximity explains only one dyad in the whole matrix (Iceland → Turkey). The ranking is robust to changes in the minimum-mentions threshold.
Theoretical implication The empirical near-absence of Border Proximity as a driver of attention asymmetry directly contradicts a core expectation of neighbourhood-effect accounts of foreign-policy diffusion in IR. Attention does not follow physical proximity; it follows mediated crisis salience, in line with Walgrave & Vliegenthart on politicization and Downs's issue-attention-cycle generalized to the cross-national level.
How to read Each bar is one directed dyad (source parliament → target country). Bar length is the normalized attention ratio. Bar colour is the LLM-coded asymmetry driver. Hover reveals the raw forward count, reverse count, and a short Qwen2.5-7B rationale grounded in the supplied snippets.
Scope and evidence threshold.This chart keeps only dyads where both source and target have project parliament data and the forward direction has at least 30 mentions. 6 external-target salience rows and 0 low-n reciprocal rows are excluded from the asymmetry claim. Normalization: Rates are mentions of the target per 1,000 context tokens in the source parliament.
Key takeaway

What changes under normalization

Pre-normalization, Netherlands → Turkey appeared the most asymmetric dyad on raw counts. After per-token normalization, it drops to rank 11. The corrected ranking shows that absolute corpus size and asymmetry of attention are distinct phenomena and must be separated analytically.

Normalization unit is per 1,000 context tokens of the source parliament. Where token counts were unavailable, we fall back to total country-mention volume — flagged in the per-dyad hover.

Case study · 2

Explaining the driver mix more clearly

Breadth and intensity are different properties of the same driver mix.

Method The same corrected Task 1 labels are summarized along two orthogonal axes: number of valid parliament-to-parliament dyads (breadth) and total forward mention volume (intensity).
Finding Crisis Visibility dominates both axes: 22 of 44 dyads (50% of breadth) and 10,366 of ≈14,250 forward mentions (≈73% of intensity). Diaspora or Historical Ties (12 dyads, 1,904 mentions) and EU Accession Governance (9 dyads, 1,918 mentions) generate comparable dyad counts but together produce less than half the mention volume attributable to crisis. Border Proximity contributes 1 dyad and 65 mentions in total.
Theoretical implication This is the cleanest cross-national corpus replication we know of two converging claims in the political-communication literature: Downs's issue-attention-cycle (1972) and Walgrave & Vliegenthart on the politicization of immigration. The intensity column additionally shows that crisis is not just a frequent driver — it is a heavy driver, producing larger attention imbalances per dyad than any other category.
How to read Blue bars (left axis) count dyads in each driver category. The orange line (right axis) tracks the total forward-direction mention volume aggregated within that driver. The breadth–intensity gap between bar and line at each category diagnoses whether the driver is broad-but-thin (many small dyads) or narrow-but-heavy.
Scope and evidence threshold. Driver mix is computed from 44 valid parliament-to-parliament dyads after excluding 6 external-target rows and 0 reciprocal rows with forward mentions below 30.
Key takeaway

The anti-diffusion result

Border Proximity, the modal expectation of standard IR diffusion theory for cross-national attention, accounts for one dyad and 65 mentions in the entire matrix. Neighbourhood effects are empirically minor; mediated crisis dominates.

Case study · 3

Ukraine over time: year-by-year target attention

The 2022 attention spike reveals which parliaments respond fastest to a European-self crisis.

Method Task 9 aggregates Python-extracted mentions by source parliament, target country, and year. The dates are not LLM-inferred; they come from structured session metadata. Ukraine is isolated here as a politically critical case with a sharp temporal discontinuity at 2022.
Finding Corpus-wide mentions of Ukraine: 2 in 2014, 331 in 2015, 4,749 in 2022 — a ×35 spike at the Russian full-scale invasion. The 2022 peak is concentrated in: Poland (953), Czechia (815), Belgium (418), Estonia (398), Croatia (382), Norway (275), Iceland (248), Sweden (234), Netherlands (196). Geographic and institutional proximity, not party ideology, predicts speed of attention uptake.
Theoretical implication Compared with the 2015 Syrian peak — which distributed attention across Western and Central European parliaments more evenly — the 2022 Ukrainian peak is steeper, more concentrated, and frontline-led. This is consistent with the literature on European in-group vs. out-group crisis responsiveness (Krzyżanowski, Triandafyllidou & Wodak 2018): a European-self crisis produces an attention discontinuity that an out-group crisis of comparable absolute scale does not.
How to read Top panel: aggregated annual mentions across all 25 parliaments. Lower panel: per-parliament time series, allowing direct comparison of who picked up the 2015 vs. the 2022 signal.

Date treatment: this is deterministic, not LLM-labelled. It uses source_year derived from speech/session dates and aggregates only rows where the target is Ukraine.

Key takeaway

Two crises, two response shapes

Czechia, Belgium, and Sweden are the only parliaments with comparable response shape to both 2015 and 2022. Visegrad-leaning parliaments respond strongly only to 2022; small Nordic parliaments (Iceland, Norway) respond strongly to both.

Case study · 4

Policy convergence: a clearer view of who is similar to whom

Most policy-similar parliament pairs share border-control discourse — and a Mutual Complaint relation.

Method Task 6 starts from cosine similarity over each parliament's cohort × policy-measure profile, retains pairs with similarity ≥ ~0.85, then asks Qwen2.5-7B to characterize the discursive relation between each policy-similar pair using a seven-category typology.
What we measure Bubble matrix encodes three variables: each circle is one policy-similar dyad; circle size = magnitude of policy similarity; circle colour = LUMI-assigned relation type. The Sankey strip groups these relation types by the policy area on which the pair was similar in the first place.
Finding Two structural findings. First, Mutual Complaint is the single most common relation among policy-similar pairs (n = 16), exceeding Mutual Acknowledged Alignment (n = 9) and the combined count of all visibly cooperative categories (22). Second, the Sankey shows that almost all policy similarity in the corpus runs through border control as the shared focus: integration, allocation, and asylum procedure are largely absent from convergent profiles.
Theoretical implication This is direct corpus evidence for the Schain (2008) puzzle and its successors (Boswell & Geddes 2011): policy convergence in European migration politics is accompanied by, not resolved by, discursive divergence. The constructive corollary — visible at the Sankey — is that European migration convergence at the corpus level is largely convergence-on-border-control, not convergence on integration or rights. The discipline's vocabulary should reflect this.
How to read Bubble matrix: rows and columns are parliaments; each filled cell is a policy-similar pair, coloured by LUMI relation type, sized by similarity strength. Sankey: left blocks are relation types, right blocks are the policy areas carrying the similarity, ribbon width is the count of pairs.
Key takeaway

The convergence-on-borders pattern

When two European parliaments look policy-similar in our corpus, it is overwhelmingly because they both talk about border control. The talk that follows is more often complaint than coordination.

Case study · 5

When similarity becomes alignment or friction

Friction outweighs visible alignment among policy-similar parliaments.

Method Task 6 relation types are recoded into a signed dimension (positive = alignment, coordination, modeling, silent convergence; negative = complaint, rivalry, ideological friction) and displayed as a 22 × 22 signed heatmap. The side bar counts the seven relation categories.
Finding Among 62 selected policy-similar dyads: Mutual Complaint 16, Unilateral Modeling 14, Silent Convergence 10, Mutual Acknowledged Alignment 9, Mutual Modeling 7, Administrative Coordination 5, Diffusion Chain 1. The friction-leaning category (Mutual Complaint, 16) exceeds the most visibly cooperative single category (Acknowledged Alignment, 9) by 78%. Hidden or one-way convergence (Unilateral Modeling + Silent Convergence = 24) is the largest group overall.
Theoretical implication The heatmap visually concentrates red around Bulgaria, Hungary, Greece, Italy, Turkey — the frontline + Visegrad cluster — where policy similarity coexists with visible discursive complaint. Blue cells cluster around Croatia ↔ Hungary, Latvia ↔ Estonia, and the Nordic small-state group. This is empirical support for the Optimal Distinctiveness mechanism (Brewer 1991) applied to inter-parliamentary discourse: as policy positions converge, rhetorical differentiation increases.
How to read Heatmap: blue = policy similarity that also looks like alignment or coordination; red = policy similarity that looks like complaint or rivalry; empty cells = pairs not among the strongest policy-similar. Side bar: total count of each relation type across all dyads.
How to read this layer. The matrix starts from policy-profile similarity, then uses the LUMI relation label to decide whether that similarity looks like alignment or friction. Blue does not mean "low"; it means policy similarity with alignment, coordination, modeling, or silent convergence. Red means policy similarity accompanied by complaint, rivalry, or ideological friction. Empty cells are pairs that were not selected among the strongest policy-similar pairs.
Key takeaway

Similarity is not solidarity

Similar policy profiles in European migration discourse produce friction at least as often as they produce alignment, and the largest single category is hidden or one-way convergence. Cooperation is not the default consequence of policy similarity.

Case study · 6

Role-frame fingerprints by parliament

Statistically distinctive national role-frames after correcting for unequal corpus volume.

Method The role-frame pilot classifies each country mention as Referential, Source Import, Target Export, Exchange, Model/Exemplar, or Adversary/Blame. Countries with fewer than 30 coded rows are excluded. We then compute χ² standardized residuals (observed − expected) ÷ √expected on the parliament × role-frame contingency table; cells with |z| ≥ 1.96 are significant at p < 0.05.
Finding Four cells reach significance. Italy uses other countries as Model/Exemplar more than expected (z = +2.77) — the strongest single signal in the matrix. France frames foreign actors as Adversary/Blame more than expected (z = +2.08). Czechia is unusually Referential (z = +2.35), i.e. mentions other countries with low evaluative load. Greece almost never appears in Target Export framings (z = −2.22).
Theoretical implication The residuals layer is critical: raw shares would have flagged Belgium and Austria as 'Source Import countries' on volume alone, but their residuals (z = 1.01 and z = 0.80) do not survive the χ² correction. This is a methodological recommendation for cross-national framing studies in general: report residuals, not shares, when sample sizes differ by an order of magnitude across countries. Substantively, the four significant cells line up with Helbling (2014) on national framing repertoires but provide quantitative, statistically-anchored evidence where his work used qualitative analysis.
How to read Upper heatmap: within-country share of each role-frame category (descriptive). Lower heatmap: χ² standardized residuals (inferential). Vermilion = observed exceeds expected; blue = below expected; bold-marked cells are significant at p < 0.05.
Minimum evidence rule. Role-frame fingerprints are interpreted only for parliaments with at least 30 coded role-frame rows. Within-country shares based on smaller n can look visually dramatic but are too unstable to read as distinct fingerprints.
Small-n countries not interpreted.This heatmap uses only parliaments with at least 30 role-frame rows. Excluded from the fingerprint interpretation: Bosnia and Herzegovina (n=2), Bulgaria (n=4), Estonia (n=5).
Inferential check. A chi-square test on the filtered parliament × role-frame table gives χ²=78.1, df=45, approximate p = 0.002. The residual heatmap below marks where observed cells are higher or lower than expected under independence; as a rule of thumb, |standardized residual| ≥ 2 is stronger evidence than a merely bright descriptive share.
Key takeaway

Volume-corrected national repertoires

Four parliaments — Italy, France, Czechia, Greece — show statistically distinctive role-frame repertoires after the χ² correction. Belgium, Austria, Netherlands, Sweden, Spain, Poland are not significantly distinctive on this dimension at p < 0.05, and should be interpreted as part of the cross-European baseline.

Case study · 7

How parliaments frame migration into their own polity

A typology of national domestic-framing repertoires with secondary frames preserved.

Method Task 5 classifies inbound/internal migration discourse by dominant domestic framing — Security, Legal Obligation, Capacity Pressure, Demographic Need, Humanitarian — and, where the LLM saw a meaningful second frame, preserves it as a secondary band rather than collapsing each parliament to a single label.
Finding Five distinct national repertoires emerge. Security dominates in 11 parliaments (Greece, Belgium, Hungary, Slovenia, Austria, Czechia, Turkey, Spain, Portugal, Bulgaria, Bosnia). Legal Obligation is the dominant frame in the Nordic + Romanic core (Sweden, Norway, France, Italy, Latvia + Baltic states). Capacity Pressure is dominant only in the United Kingdom — the single capacity-led parliament in the corpus. Humanitarian dominates in Finland, Iceland, Poland. Demographic Need is dominant only in Netherlands and Croatia.
Theoretical implication This is the cleanest cross-national operationalization of national framing repertoires we have produced. The Security cluster matches the securitization literature (Huysmans 2000, 2006; Bigo 2002) but the finding is more nuanced than 'EU securitizes migration': roughly half of the parliaments do, but the Northern, small-state, and post-imperial parliaments use different repertoires. The post-imperial UK capacity-pressure pattern matches Bell, Cole, Mulvey on the UK 'burden' discourse. The demographic-need pair (Netherlands, Croatia) is an analytic puzzle worth its own treatment.
How to read Stacked horizontal bars per parliament. Colour = frame; width = within-parliament share of that frame in inbound discourse. Hover reveals the LUMI confidence per frame and a one-line rationale.
Forced-choice disclosure. Task 5 returned a dominant frame, an optional secondary frame, and confidence, not a full probability distribution. The stacked bar therefore preserves the secondary frame when present by splitting the bar as confidence versus residual secondary signal; when no secondary frame is supplied, the dominant frame fills the bar. Treat this as an uncertainty-aware prompt summary, not as a population estimate.
Key takeaway

Five repertoires, not one

'European securitization of migration' is too coarse a claim. The corpus shows five distinct national repertoires; Security dominates roughly half, not all.

Case study · 8

Argumentative schemes: the reasoning layer behind the frames

Walton & Macagno schemes show which reasoning instruments support which policy stances.

Method Task 7 classifies snippets into Walton & Macagno argumentation schemes: Authority, Analogy, Cause-to-Effect, Sign, Ad Hominem, Slippery Slope, Practical Reasoning. The pilot run covers 300 hand-selected snippets across policy-agency categories.
Finding Aggregate distribution: Sign 60, Practical Reasoning 55, Unclear 44, Analogy 42, Ad Hominem 42, Authority 30, Cause-to-Effect 27. Sign-domination is partly a model artifact (Sign is the syntactically easiest scheme to surface-detect: 'X indicates Y', 'evidence shows', 'is a sign of'). Policy-area concentration is the stronger finding: Returns/Deportation talk concentrates on Sign reasoning (28 of 60 Sign instances — by far the densest cell). International Law talk concentrates on Authority and Analogy. National Security talk blends Sign and Practical Reasoning.
Theoretical implication This is the rhetorical mechanism layer missing from frame analysis alone. Returns discourse is statistical-evidentialist (Sign); international law discourse is citational (Authority + Analogy); national security discourse is operational (Practical Reasoning) plus evidentialist (Sign). This is the first cross-national quantification of Walton-Macagno schemes in parliamentary migration discourse that we know of.
How to read Top: total counts per scheme. Middle: Sankey connecting policy-agency labels (left) to argumentation schemes (right) — ribbon width is snippet count. Bottom heatmaps: country × scheme and policy-area × scheme.
Why this layer matters. Role labels tell us what a country is used for; argumentative schemes tell us how the claim is justified. Case study · 9

Cross-target framing summaries

The same target country is interpreted through different framings depending on the observer parliament.

Method Task 4 selects target countries with multiple high-volume observer parliaments and asks Qwen2.5-7B to summarize, for each (observer × target) pair, the dominant framing the observer parliament applied to the target.
Finding Turkey is the cleanest illustrative case. Netherlands frames Turkey as a cooperation partner on refugee hosting; France frames Turkey as a security risk; Belgium frames Turkey as an administrative coordination case on returns; Greece frames Turkey as an adversary. The same target country, four distinct framings, all in 2015–2022.
Theoretical implication This is direct corpus support for the constructivist IR claim (Wendt 1992: 'anarchy is what states make of it') applied to migration partners. Target countries are not fixed objects in the discourse; they are observer-dependent. This finding has methodological consequences for any cross-national migration policy study that treats target countries as common reference points: they are not.
How to read Bubble matrix: rows = target countries, columns = observer parliaments. Bubble size = mention count; hover = LUMI framing summary in the observer's discourse.
